Thailand’s electrification:
who pays,
who gains?

Thailand needs much more electricity, and much of the new demand comes from data centres that want clean power. Whether this journey narrows or widens the gap between rich and poor depends on rules that are still being written. We grade each claim by its evidence, so you can see what is known and what is still a question.

How to read this page

Each finding carries a grade. Supported: official data or documents back it. Partly supported: the facts exist but the conclusion is one reading among several. Not yet evidenced: a fair question, but no public evidence yet. Figures are as of 27 October 2026, before the official rules of the 50,000-baht scheme were published. This page names no person or company as acting in bad faith; it shows where to look.

Rising demand, and data centres

Supported. Data centres worldwide used about 415 TWh in 2024, roughly twice Thailand’s total use, and the IEA expects about 945 TWh by 2030. Thailand’s planned data centres (about 2.87 GW) could add roughly 10% to national demand at 80% use (our estimate). Few permanent jobs come with them, and they compete for water and grid upgrades. Full detail: Data centres: the real costs and benefits.

Why data centres want clean power

Partly supported. Thailand has no general law that every data centre must use a set share of clean power. The expectation comes from three places:

SourceWhat it says
Company pledgesLarge cloud firms buy renewable power equal to their yearly use; Google aims for carbon-free power every hour by 2030 (about 65% hour by hour in 2020)
GermanyData centres of 300 kW or more: 50% unsubsidised renewable power from 2024, 100% from 2027
Thailand, direct PPA (draft, Oct 2025)Buyer: a BOI-promoted data centre of at least 50 MW that intends 100% renewable power. Seller: a new renewable plant of at least 1,000 kVA. Opened to all industries in Aug 2026 (reported)
Thailand, green tariffsUGT1 adds certificates from existing hydro plants; UGT2 costs about 4.56 baht/kWh for 10 years from new solar, wind and biogas

The reasons: climate pledges and emissions reporting, customers’ supply-chain rules (including EU carbon rules for exporters), long-term price stability, and competition between countries for investment. The gap to watch: about 30% of data-centre power worldwide still comes from coal, and Thailand’s Energy Minister has said operators publicly demand 100% clean energy yet often ask for “any power immediately”.

A fair share: who gets to sell clean power?

Supported on access; partly supported on profit. The direct-sale rules are written for large plants, so a household rooftop cannot take part. Per unit, households and big solar farms are paid about the same; the difference is scale and contract length.

One large listed company signed 25-year contracts with EGAT for 12 solar projects totalling 644.8 MW under the 2022 scheme; a household gets at most 5 kW. The larger money is in fossil capacity: Thailand’s reserve margin was 34–38% in 2025 against a 15% norm, and under some private contracts availability payments are made even when plants are not used, costs that TDRI says flow into tariffs.

What a fairer system could look like: reserve a share of data-centre green-power demand for households, cooperatives and community solar; let aggregators pool many rooftops to reach the 1,000 kVA threshold; publish the full chain of prices; and give households contracts as long as large producers get.

The 200,000-baht tax deduction: who benefits?

Supported: it favours higher incomes. It reduces taxable income, so its value rises with the tax bracket: about 6,100–50,000 baht saved on the full 200,000 baht (SCB EIC). Rules: new on-grid systems up to 10 kWp, installed 3 March 2026 – 31 December 2028, claimant must own the meter and hold an e-tax invoice from a VAT-registered seller.

EffectWho gains or loses
Tax saved grows with incomeTop earners gain most; people below the tax threshold gain nothing
Meter must be in the claimant’s nameRenters and families with the meter in a parent’s name are left out

Side effects to watch: a 2026–2028 rush means panels reaching end of life together while recycling is still being planned, and deadline pressure invites poor installations.

The 50,000-baht scheme: what is still unclear

“Unclear” is supported; a 25-year debt trap is not supported by the terms reported so far; hidden intent is not yet evidenced. Reported terms: 50,000 baht for each of 1 million homes; PEA’s example of a 5 kW system at about 140,000 baht leaves about 90,000 baht; loans at 2.5% for 7 years; surplus bought at 2.20 baht. Still unclear: the vendor list and its criteria; the interest rate after the promotion and the penalty for missed payments; whether the subsidy goes to the installer or the household; the buy-back term (10 or 20 years); how the 90,000-baht balance is financed; and whether batteries and ground-mounted systems get the same terms.

Does a loan pay for itself? Our arithmetic: 90,000 baht at 2.5% over 7 years ≈ 1,169 baht a month. A 5 kW system makes about 600 units a month.

The poorest households pay the lowest tariff, use little power in the day and depend on selling at 2.20 baht, so their margin is thin; a cloudy month, a rate rise or an inverter fault can push them below zero. Why the rules are unclear has ordinary explanations (Cabinet approves money first, then regulators write rules; political timing), and the vendor list, once published, will show who benefits.

What people say

We read about 175 public Facebook comments on four widely shared posts: clear worries outnumbered support about six to one. The main worries were debt, distrust of who supplies the equipment, the low 2.20-baht buy-back price and rising installation prices; supporters said it pays for homes that use power in the daytime. Full summary and a reader poll: What people on Facebook are saying.

Agnotology, and being realistic

A flood of complex detail – panel types, IEC and Thai standards, sizes and scales, the positive published and the negative left out – can leave the public confused enough to give up. Researchers call the deliberate production of confusion agnotology. The answer is not to be negative or positive, but realistic: ask who pays, who gains, for how long, and who checks. There is no free lunch: the 50,000 baht is borrowed public money, the deduction is tax given up, and grid upgrades are paid through tariffs.

  • Is the full rulebook published, with dates?
  • Can I choose my own installer, and is the vendor list public?
  • What is the loan rate after the promotion, and the penalty if I miss a payment?
  • Is the buy-back price fixed, and for how many years?
  • Does my daytime use make the numbers work without relying on selling power?
  • Who supplies the equipment, and is the warranty in writing?
