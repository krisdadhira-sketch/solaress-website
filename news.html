฿50,000 toward solar for one million Thai homes: where the plan stands

A government plan to pay up to 50,000 baht toward a solar system for each of one million households has cleared a key screening board and is due before Cabinet on 26 October. Here is what has been reported so far, and what we suggest you do while the rules are finalised.

A summary of reporting by The Nation (20 October 2026 and 2 October 2026), The Thaiger (20 October 2026) and Pattaya Mail (20 September 2026). All credit for the reporting goes to these outlets. The notes for customers are ours.

Subsidy per householdup to ฿50,000
Households, phase one1 million
Sell-back, net billing฿2.20/kWh
  • Where it stands. The loan-spending screening board has approved the Interior Ministry’s 50-billion-baht proposal, paid from the energy-transition part of the government’s emergency borrowing. It is not final until Cabinet approves it, expected on 26 October.
  • Who and how much. One subsidy per household, up to 50,000 baht toward installation. Reports say applicants only need to be a registered electricity customer (a meter in their name); the final rules are still to be published.
  • What can be installed. The earlier proposal allows systems of up to 10 kW, on a roof, on open ground or floating on a pond. Only up to 5 kW can be sold back to the grid.
  • Selling surplus power. Surplus is bought at 2.20 baht per unit under net billing. The National Energy Policy Council raised the programme’s purchase target to 10,000 MW in September and extended the purchase period to 20 years (the earlier round was 10 years). Participating homes are to get a smart meter free of charge.
  • Loans for the rest. Government Savings Bank, Government Housing Bank and BAAC are expected to offer solar loans, reported at 2.5% a year over seven years without collateral, with sell-back income paying the loan down.
  • Who may install. The October proposal says installers must meet international standards, employ qualified engineers, be financially stable and be on the electricity authorities’ official vendor list.
  • When. Registration has been reported as opening 1 November or around mid-November; no date is confirmed. A second phase of 500,000 homes (25 billion baht) has been proposed but not approved.

What it means for you: a 50,000-baht grant covers a large part of a typical 3–5 kW home system, so this could be the best time in years to go solar. But do not sign or pay a deposit to anyone promising the subsidy until the rules and application channel are officially announced; schemes like this attract cowboy installers, and the government plans to allow only listed vendors. What you can do now: check that the electricity meter is in your name, keep a recent bill, and find out whether your supply is single-phase or three-phase. Officials estimate a 5 kW system could earn about 1,000 baht a month from surplus sales; that is a projection, and at 2.20 baht per unit, power you use yourself is still worth more than power you sell, so we size for daytime self-use first. Book a consultation and we will tell you as soon as the application rules are out.

Solar panels sold in Thailand must soon carry a mandatory safety standard (มอก.)

The Ministry of Industry is making the solar panel safety standard compulsory, with seven more standards in preparation for cables, batteries, inverters and protection gear. Here is what changes, and why it matters if you are buying a system.

A summary of “Thailand sets October deadline for solar panel safety standards”, Pattaya Mail, 17 October 2026, and “Thailand’s TISI Race Heats Up: Factory Audits Could Squeeze Compliant PV Module Supply” by Ryan Tey Tze Yang, Shanghai Metals Market (SMM), 27 August 2026. All credit for the reporting goes to Pattaya Mail and SMM. The Thailand notes are ours.

Maker / importer, max. fine฿2 million
Seller, max. fine฿500,000
More standards coming7
  • The standard. TIS 61730 Part 2-2567, aligned with the international IEC 61730-2, covers the electrical, mechanical and fire safety of a panel. It has existed as a voluntary standard since June 2025; it now becomes compulsory, taking effect once published in the Royal Gazette, which the Industry Minister ordered for October.
  • What is tested. Heat resistance, safe electrical disconnection, performance at high temperature, protection against electrical leakage, resistance to fire spreading from heat or sparks, and mechanical strength.
  • Who must comply. Manufacturers, importers and sellers may only deal in certified panels. Making or importing non-compliant panels can bring up to 2 years in jail and a 2-million-baht fine; selling them, up to 6 months and 500,000 baht.
  • Next in line. Seven more standards are being prepared: DC cable, lithium batteries, fuses, inverters, DC circuit breakers, connectors and combiner boxes.
  • A possible short squeeze. SMM notes that TISI inspects each factory that supplies Thailand separately, and an IEC certificate does not convert automatically into a Thai licence. If the rule starts before enough factories are approved, certified panels could be short for a while. SMM saw no clear price premium for certified panels as of August.

What it means for you: this is good news for buyers. Burnt and melted systems found in this year’s consumer investigations (see our 17 October story) came largely from substandard parts and wiring. When you compare quotations, ask that every panel carries the มอก. mark and that the contract names the brand, model and standard of each item. The panels we propose are Tier-1 brands with IEC 61215 / 61730 certificates; we will confirm the มอก. licence on every quotation as the rule takes effect, and keep customers informed if supply tightens. Thailand plans a recycling estate for old solar panels and EV batteries

The first big wave of Thai solar panels is nearing the end of its working life. The Ministry of Industry wants a dedicated, controlled estate to recycle them, together with EV batteries and e-waste, and much tougher fines for illegal dumping.

A summary of “Thailand prepares for surge in solar and EV battery waste with new recycling estate”, The Nation, 17 October 2026. All credit for the original article goes to The Nation. The Thailand notes are ours.

  • Why now. Panels installed about ten years ago are approaching the roughly 15-year service life the ministry assumes, so large volumes could reach the waste stream within one to two years. EV batteries are a growing second stream as Thailand’s EV fleet expands.
  • The plan. One controlled area for recycling plants and hazardous-waste treatment, with central wastewater treatment and 24-hour monitoring, so panels, batteries and e-waste stay out of ordinary rubbish. No location, size, budget or opening date has been given.
  • Second life first. Some used EV batteries can be reconditioned as energy storage before final recycling.
  • Tougher penalties. Some current fines stop at 200,000 baht. The ministry wants them raised to tens of millions of baht by amending the Factory Act and industrial-waste law, and to trace and seize the assets of illegal dumpers, working with the environment ministry, DSI and Customs.

What it means for you: a good panel lasts far longer than 15 years; quality panels carry 25–30-year power warranties, and most early failures we see come from installation, heat and dirt rather than the panel itself. So for most owners, recycling is a long way off. What helps now: buy panels that last, keep them clean and checked (see our cleaning and maintenance jobs), and when a panel or battery is replaced, make sure it goes to a licensed recycler, not the roadside. Ask us when you upgrade and we will handle the old equipment properly.

22 October 2026 · Energy policy

Who controls the energy transition? Key points from TNI’s State of Power 2024

A 165-page report by the Transnational Institute looks at who owns, finances and profits from the move to clean energy, with case studies from four continents. Here are the main points in short, with a note on what they mean for solar in Thailand.

A summary of “Energy, Power and Transition: State of Power 2024”, Transnational Institute (TNI), edited by Nick Buxton, February 2024. All credit for the research goes to TNI and the report’s authors. The Thailand notes are ours.

  • An expansion, not yet a transition. Renewables are growing fast, but fossil fuels still supply about two-thirds of world energy. Oil and gas firms earned a record of about US$4 trillion in 2022, and most of that cash went to shareholders and debt repayment, with only a small share for clean energy.
  • Who uses energy. Industry takes almost half of the world’s electricity, while households use about a quarter. Sub-Saharan Africa is the only region where the number of people without electricity has grown since 2000, to about 589 million.
  • Private renewables, public risk. In Africa, “de-risking” deals use public guarantees and fixed power-purchase contracts so that private investors carry little risk, while states and communities carry the rest. South Africa’s US$8.5 billion transition package is mostly loans, with very little in grants.
  • Big solar and wind can leave locals behind. Case studies from India (the 2 GW Pavagada solar park), Germany (Brandenburg) and South Australia show large projects bringing cheap power but few local jobs, and land owners gaining far more than landless villagers.
  • Citizen ownership works. In Germany, citizens, cooperatives and farmers held about 40% of renewable capacity by 2019, thanks to feed-in tariffs. That share is now falling as banks and big utilities take over.
  • Public options are coming back. New York passed a law in 2023 for its public power authority to build renewables, aiming for 70% renewable power by 2030. Germany saw 284 energy re-municipalisations between 2005 and 2017.
  • Small fixes, big gaps. In Lebanon, homes switched to solar panels and batteries during blackouts, but only those who could afford them. The authors also warn of future waste from old panels and batteries without a plan to handle it.
  • Waste-to-energy is a weak answer. Burning waste supplies under 3% of Europe’s energy and emits about 1.2 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of waste. Long contracts that guarantee a minimum amount of waste can block recycling. The report notes Thailand plans 79 such plants.
  • Community energy in practice. Across Latin America, over 100 community projects use solar panels, small hydro and biogas run by local people, with women often leading.
  • The report’s main call. Put the transition under more public and community control: fair land deals, revenue-sharing, local jobs and training, and grants rather than debt for poorer countries.

What it means in Thailand: the report’s strongest point for us is that energy owned by the people who use it keeps the benefits local. Rooftop solar on a home, resort, school or factory is exactly that: the owner keeps the savings, and the money stays in the community rather than flowing to a distant investor. It also echoes what we see on site: good design and local, qualified installers matter as much as the panels. The report is written from a strongly public-ownership viewpoint and some of its proposals are political; we share it for the facts and case studies, so readers can judge for themselves. Silicon-rich battery cells pass 1,000 cycles: what it means for solar storage

Germany’s Fraunhofer ISE has built lighter, more energy-dense lithium cells with a silicon-rich anode. Here is the news in short, with a note on what it means for home and resort batteries in Thailand.

A summary of “Fraunhofer: silicon-rich cells retain over 83% after 1,000 cycles”, pv magazine, 21 October 2026. All credit for the original article goes to pv magazine. The Thailand notes are ours.

  • The cells. 25 Ah pouch cells whose anode is a silicon-carbon and graphite blend, with silicon-carbon making up about half of the active material, paired with a nickel-rich NMC cathode. They were made on a semi-automatic line using commercially available electrode foils.
  • The result. More than 83% of capacity kept after over 1,000 full cycles at 1C, with an energy density of 280 Wh/kg in a research format. Fraunhofer expects about 330 Wh/kg once the format is optimised for industry.
  • The project. Part of the German StrOboBatt project led by PCC Thorion with Fraunhofer ISE and Glatt Ingenieurtechnik, with LG Chem Europe among the partners and €2.6 million of public funding. The main target is stationary storage of solar and wind power, with electric vehicles and aerospace also named.
  • The caveats. The 83% figure only holds when the cells are kept under high mechanical pressure; at lower pressure the capacity fell much faster, and no figure was published. Usable capacity during normal cycling also drifted below the slow-test capacity as the cells aged. This is research work with no launch date.

What it means in Thailand: the big win of silicon anodes is weight, which matters for cars and aircraft, not for a battery bolted to a wall. For home, homestay and resort storage, lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) remains far ahead on the things that count: the batteries we install are rated for more than 6,000 cycles, against about 1,000 cycles to 83% here, and LiFePO4 copes better with heat in a Thai plant room than nickel-rich NMC. Our battery advice does not change; see Our picks: Battery. We will keep watching silicon-anode cells for EV charging and portable storage.

A record 93% bifacial solar panel: what it means for Thai homes and carports

Tongwei says its new TNC 3.0 “Bifimax” panel turns light on its back side into power almost as well as on its front. Here is the news in short, with a note on where it matters in Thailand.

A summary of “Tongwei claims record-breaking 93.07% bifaciality factor for 770 W solar module”, pv magazine, 17 July 2026. All credit for the original article goes to pv magazine. The Thailand notes are ours.

  • The record. TÜV Rheinland verified a bifaciality of about 93%: the back of the panel produces about 93% of what the front produces from the same light. Most bifacial panels today reach 80–88%.
  • The panel. A 770 W TOPCon module with 24.8% efficiency, losing about 0.26% of its power for each degree of heat.
  • How. Tongwei credits a new rear-side texture and an improved “zebra-crossing” contact design; the cell itself reaches 94.3% bifaciality.
  • The caveats. This is a certified record, not yet a product on sale: the article does not say when it will be mass-produced, and in 2025 Tongwei itself called 90% bifaciality in mass production “a major challenge”. The extra-income figures for large solar farms are company estimates.

What it means in Thailand: extra output from the back of a panel only comes when light can reach it. On a normal roof the back is in shade, so a bifacial panel adds very little. The gain is real on raised carports, A-frames and ground mounts, especially over light-coloured ground, gravel or a white roof. For most home and shop roofs our advice does not change: single-sided back-contact panels give the most power per square metre. For open sites, high-bifaciality TOPCon panels are becoming even more attractive. See our 650–670 W panel comparison to choose the right panel for your site.

18 October 2026 · Buying advice

The honest downsides of solar, and how they look in Thailand

A UK solar group-buying scheme has listed seven real drawbacks of going solar. Here they are in short, with a note on how each one plays out for homes and businesses in Thailand.

A summary of “The Honest Disadvantages of Solar Energy” by Mathew Williams, Switch Together (iChoosr Ltd), 13 January 2026. All credit for the original article goes to Switch Together. The Thailand notes are ours.

  • No sun, no power. Panels make nothing at night and less on cloudy days, so solar alone cannot run a home around the clock; a battery fills the gap. In Thailand: sunshine is strong all year and most homes and businesses use the most power in the daytime, so far more of the output is used straight away. A battery is worth it mainly for evening use or backup during outages.
  • It needs space. A useful system needs a fair area of roof or ground, and a battery needs room too. In Thailand: roofs are usually big enough; the bigger questions are roof strength, shade from trees or buildings, and which way the roof faces.
  • The upfront cost. Solar is a medium- to long-term investment rather than an instant saving, although prices have fallen fast. In Thailand: payback is often a few years for daytime users. Try our savings calculator to see the price for your system size.
  • Upkeep. Rain helps, but the article suggests a professional clean every 1 to 2 years and a fuller check every 4 to 5 years. In Thailand: dust, smoke-haze season and bird droppings cut output faster, so a yearly clean and system check pays for itself. We run project based cleaning and maintenance jobs at every system we look after.
  • Battery waste. Batteries thrown away carelessly can pollute soil and water. They can be recycled, and they last many years. In Thailand: ask who will take the battery back at the end of its life before you buy.
  • Looks. Some people dislike how panels look, so placement matters. In Thailand: modern all-black panels and careful layout help a lot.
  • The technology keeps improving. Early buyers can feel left behind, but today’s panels already cut bills and emissions well. In Thailand: waiting for “better” panels means paying full electricity bills in the meantime.

What it means for you: none of these is a reason to avoid solar, but each one is a question to ask before you sign: how much of the power will you use yourself, is the roof strong and unshaded, who cleans and checks the system, and who takes care of the battery at the end of its life? Thailand: consumer board investigates faulty solar installations

Thailand’s Office of the Consumer Protection Board (OCPB) has opened an investigation into substandard home solar installations, after homeowners reported melting and burning equipment and electricity bills that did not go down.

A summary of reporting by pv magazine, “Thailand opens investigation into faulty solar installations” by Patrick Jowett, 19 May 2026, with details from Thai press (Thairath, Thai PBS, InfoQuest). All credit for the reporting goes to pv magazine and the Thai news outlets listed below.

Households being helped17
Reported losses500k+ ฿
Areas affected3
  • What happened. The OCPB is assisting 17 homeowners in Khon Kaen, Saraburi and the Tha Phra area of Bangkok after their solar systems showed signs of melting and burning. Thai press put the losses at more than 500,000 baht.
  • Bills did not drop. Homeowners told a meeting chaired by Minister Supamas Isarabhakdi that their bills stayed the same after installation, and that the installer would not take responsibility.
  • What went wrong. Early checks found sub-standard equipment, aluminium cable used in place of copper, and a three-phase supply changed to single-phase. The result was voltage drops, outages, melted parts and a real fire risk. One case has gone from the police to the public prosecutor.
  • Possible criminal charges. The board says cases like this may count as selling deceptive goods, which can carry jail time and fines. Product labelling breaches alone can cost a seller up to 6 months in jail or a 100,000 baht fine, and a manufacturer or importer up to 1 year or 200,000 baht.
  • New rules on the way. The minister asked the board to make solar installation a “regulated contract business”, so every contract must state the equipment standards and specifications. She also wants labelling rules for panels, inverters and batteries.
  • Who is involved. Officials met the Thai Industrial Standards Institute (TISI), the Metropolitan Electricity Authority (MEA) and the Provincial Electricity Authority (PEA) to stop this happening again. Consumers with similar problems can complain to the OCPB by hotline (1166) or online.

What it means for you: before you sign, ask any installer, including us, four questions. Is all cabling copper? Will your supply stay as it is (three-phase stays three-phase)? Does the contract name the brand, model and standard (มอก./IEC) of every item? And who is responsible if something goes wrong? We are happy to put all of this in writing. 16 October 2026 · Environment

How clean is solar power, really?

The Union of Concerned Scientists (UCS), a US science non-profit, has updated its review of the environmental impacts of solar power. Its answer: solar is not impact-free, but its footprint is small next to coal and gas, and most of it can be reduced by choosing the right site and building panels that last.

A summary of “Environmental Impacts of Solar Power”, Union of Concerned Scientists, first published 5 March 2013 and last updated 4 March 2026. All credit for the research goes to the Union of Concerned Scientists.

Lower CO₂ than gas, life cycle−91%
Lower CO₂ than coal, life cycle−96%
Typical panel life30+ yrs
  • Land.. Rooftop solar uses no extra land. Large US solar farms use about 2.9–4.2 acres (1.2–1.7 ha) per megawatt. Building on degraded land, old mines, or along roads and power lines reduces the impact, and so does sharing land: solar over crops (agrivoltaics) or floating on reservoirs and ponds (floating solar), which also cuts evaporation.
  • Wildlife.. Clearing land can fragment habitats and release carbon stored in the soil. Using land that is already disturbed, putting panels on roofs and over car parks, and planting native, pollinator-friendly plants under the panels all help.
  • Water.. Solar panels use no water to make electricity; only manufacturing uses some. Washing panels can raise output, though in the US it is often not worth the cost.
  • Materials.. Silicon panels are about 87% glass and aluminium; thin-film CdTe panels are over 90%. Lead (in silicon panels) and cadmium (in CdTe panels) are each under 0.1%. An International Energy Agency study found low health risk even in a worst-case unlined landfill, and a 2023 study found no arsenic, gallium, germanium, hexavalent chromium or PFAS. Factories do handle strong acids, so worker safety matters.
  • Waste.. US panel waste to 2050 is estimated at 54–160 million tonnes. Coal ash would be 300–800 times larger. Recycling is not yet common because it often costs more than the materials are worth, so the best lever is longer-lasting panels: 10% longer life cuts life-cycle waste by 53%.
  • Climate.. Making electricity from sunlight gives off no greenhouse gases. Counting manufacturing, transport, installation and disposal, solar PV emits about 91% less CO₂-equivalent than gas power and 96% less than coal (NREL, 2021).
  • Concentrating solar (CSP).. Mirror-based thermal plants use more land (5–10 acres per MW), need cooling water unless dry-cooled, and their tower designs can harm birds and insects. They are a very small share of solar.

What it means for you: a rooftop system is the low-impact end of solar: no extra land, no water, and power that stays clean for decades. We fit Tier-1 panels built to last 25–30 years.

Rooftop solar in Thailand: what helps, what holds it back

CASE for Southeast Asia, a regional energy-transition programme, has published a review of home rooftop solar in Thailand. Its conclusion: the technology is proven. What slows it down now is cost, roof space, how exported power is paid for, and what happens to panels at the end of their life.

A summary of “Unlocking Thailand’s Solar Potential: Addressing Challenges for Clean Energy Deployment” by Palita Srichaisuphakit, CASE for Southeast Asia, published 12 June 2026. All credit for the research goes to the author and CASE for Southeast Asia.

Sell-back rate, 10 years฿2.20/kWh
Income tax deduction, to Dec 2028฿200,000
Typical 3 kW payback6–7 yrs
  • The support that exists. Under the People's Solar scheme, households can sell surplus solar power to PEA or MEA at THB 2.20 per kWh for 10 years. Households can also deduct up to THB 200,000 of the cost of a home rooftop system from taxable income until December 2028. Because it is a deduction and not a refund, how much it saves depends on the owner's tax bracket.
  • Upfront cost. The smallest common system is about 3 kW. It makes roughly 300–400 kWh a month and pays for itself in about six to seven years. A battery for evening use makes the system more independent, but it adds cost and lengthens the payback.
  • Roofs and condos. The roof must be strong enough and face the sun. People in condominiums and apartments usually cannot install at all. Letting buyers take solar power from elsewhere through the grid (third-party access) would help them. Thailand's 2,000 MW Direct PPA pilot does this, but it is only for large users such as data centres, not homes.
  • Net billing versus net metering. Thailand pays for exported power in money (net billing), at a lower rate than the price you pay for grid power. Under net metering, each exported unit would cancel one unit you buy, which would shorten payback. The review notes the other side as well: grid stability, and network costs moving onto households without solar.
  • Panels at end of life. Panels last about 25–30 years. Thailand could have about 9,900–57,200 tonnes of used panels by 2030, and it has no full system yet for collecting and recycling them. The review suggests making producers responsible and setting up reuse and recycling.

Update: the sell-back scheme has reopened. The review notes that the old 90 MW quota closed in June 2024. Since then, the Energy Regulatory Commission has opened a new round of 500 MW at the same THB 2.20 per kWh for 10 years, with applications from 1 July 2026, first come, first served. Up to 5 kW per meter can be sold (a larger system is fine for your own use), batteries are allowed, and the system must be supplying the grid by 2027. In Chiang Rai and other PEA areas, apply through PEA's PPIM system.

What it means for you: at THB 2.20, power you use yourself is worth much more than power you sell. So we still size systems for daytime self-use first and treat sell-back as a bonus for the surplus. When the load fell, the solar fell too: zero export in practice

A 20 kWp solar plant we look after in Chiang Rai lost two buildings from its supply on 1 October. Over the next 15 days its electricity use fell 42%, and its solar production fell 21%, even though the weather was better than in late September. The reason is a setting many owners choose without knowing what it costs: zero export.

Why the solar fell. The plant has no battery, and export to the grid is switched off in the inverter. So the inverter can only produce what the building is using at that moment. On busy days, when the load reached 180–320 kWh, the panels delivered 50–58 kWh, close to what the season allows. On quiet days the load was only about 46 kWh and the panels gave about 31 kWh. On Sundays, with the site closed, they gave almost nothing. About 265 kWh of free solar went unused in the first half of October alone.

Zero export still leaked. Even with export off, about 14% of the solar still reached the grid. Most of the load sat on one of the three phases, while the inverter feeds all three evenly, so the lightly used phases sent power out while the busy phase drew power in. Without a sell-back agreement, that energy earns nothing.

What it means for you: if your system is set to zero export, check what happens on your quiet days. Balancing the load across the three phases, running flexible loads such as air conditioning or water pumps at midday, a PEA sell-back agreement or a small battery can all turn unused sunshine into savings. We can read your monitoring data and tell you which fits your site.

Grounding a solar system: why one earth point is not enough

New research from Indonesia shows that adding a second grounding point to a solar array can cut the voltage spike from a direct lightning strike by roughly half. We read the study alongside other recent research, the international lightning standards and the Thai wiring code. Here is what it adds up to.

Voltage cut with a 2nd point43–55%
Soil 30 → 3,000 ΩmHigher spikes
Best earth pointNearest the strike

What the study found. Researchers at Institut Teknologi Sepuluh Nopember and Universitas Hasanuddin simulated a 50 kA lightning strike on a section of ten panels. They compared earthing through the panel frames with earthing through the mounting structure, moved the earth point around, and tried one versus two earth connections. Where the earth point sits, and how many there are, mattered more than whether the frames or the structure carried the current.

Frames earthed: voltage reduction with a second earth point
47.5–54.8%
Mounting structure earthed: voltage reduction with a second earth point
43.2–50.7%

Earthing through the mounting structure gave slightly lower peaks because its path has lower resistance. An earth point close to where lightning strikes gave the lowest voltage, because the current has a shorter way to go. In poor, dry or rocky soil (3,000 Ωm against 30 Ωm), every voltage rose. The study is a computer simulation and does not cover damage to cables or inverters, so it shows the direction, not exact numbers for a real site.

What other research and the standards add.

  • Ground-mounted arrays need a mesh, not just a rod. A Graz University of Technology study of an 84 m × 84 m solar farm found that strikes at the corners give the highest step voltages, that a tighter bonding mesh works better, and that meeting the common 10 Ω earth-resistance figure on its own does not guarantee safe step voltages in high-resistivity soil.
  • Bond first, then earth. All panel frames, rails and metal parts should be joined into one continuous system and connected to the building’s main earth terminal with a low-resistance link. The DC wires themselves are not earthed; the inverter watches their insulation instead.
  • Lightning rods change the design. If the building has a lightning protection system, the array should either sit inside its protected zone at the required separation distance, or, if that distance cannot be kept, be bonded to it with conductors that can carry lightning current and protected by Type 1 surge arresters on the DC side. Industry engineers warn that simply clamping the array to the lightning tape can send strike current down the DC cables to the inverter.
  • Surge protection on both sides. Fit SPDs on the DC and AC sides as close to the inverter as possible, and add another set when the cable between the arrester and the equipment runs longer than about 10 m. Long DC runs are better in metal tray or armoured cable than in plastic conduit.
  • Thai rules. The EIT wiring standard (2556) calls for earth rods at least 16 mm in diameter and 2.40 m long, driven at least 2.40 m into the ground, sets 5 Ω as the limit where a metal building structure is earthed, and sizes the earthing conductor by the main cable (at least 10 mm² copper for main conductors up to 35 mm²).

The approach we recommend

  1. Bond every frame and rail into one continuous system with proper earthing clamps, and connect it to the building’s main earth.
  2. On larger roofs and ground mounts, use more than one earth connection, placed at the ends and corners of the array, with short, straight paths to the ground.
  3. Measure earth resistance at handover. In dry or rocky soil, add rods or a ring electrode until the reading is acceptable.
  4. Fit SPDs on both the DC and AC sides at the inverter, and add another set near the array when the cable run is long.
  5. Run DC plus and minus cables together to avoid loops, and use metal tray or conduit for long runs.
  6. Where there is a lightning rod system, design for the separation distance or full lightning bonding. Never just tie the array to the lightning tape.
  7. Inspect once a year: loose clamps, corrosion and SPD status indicators.

What it means for you: good earthing is cheap compared with a burnt inverter. If your system has a single earth wire, or you are not sure how it was earthed, ask us to check it. This is work for a qualified electrician, and the relevant standards and the equipment makers’ instructions have the final word. Our SPD choices are on the Our picks page.

14 October 2026 · Safety

The breaker that worked fine until the solar went in

An RCBO is the small breaker that protects a circuit against earth leakage, overload and short circuit in one unit. Most people never think about which way power flows through it. Once a home has solar, a battery or a generator, that question decides whether the breaker will actually trip.

A familiar story. A house has been wired for years with ordinary RCBOs. Power always came one way: from the grid, through the main switch, through each RCBO and out to the lights and sockets. Then a hybrid inverter and battery are added, and the backup circuit is fed from the inverter. Everything switches on and seems to work. But on that circuit, power can now come in from the side the breaker was never designed to be fed from.

Why it matters. A unidirectional RCBO is built for power flowing one way only, from its Line (In) terminals to its Load (Out) terminals. Fed backwards from the load side, it may not trip on a fault, can be damaged, and can leave its internal electronics live while someone thinks the circuit is safe. A bidirectional RCBO is tested and certified by its maker to break a fault and detect leakage whichever way the power is flowing.

How to tell which one you have. Look at the case and the small wiring diagram on the front. Terminals marked Line / Load or In / Out, or an arrow, mean unidirectional. A bidirectional RCBO has no fixed supply side, or its datasheet says plainly that it is suitable for power flowing in both directions. If the datasheet does not say bidirectional, treat it as unidirectional.

How it should be wired.

  • On a unidirectional RCBO, the incoming supply goes only to the Line / In terminals and the circuit goes to Load / Out.
  • Never connect an inverter output, battery feed or generator to the Load side of a unidirectional RCBO.
  • On any circuit that solar, a battery, a generator or a V2G charger can feed, fit an RCBO the maker certifies as bidirectional, and wire it to the maker's diagram.
  • Where the neutral must be switched as well, use a double-pole device.

What it means for you: if you have added solar or a battery to an existing board, ask your installer which RCBOs sit on the circuits the inverter can feed, and whether they are bidirectional. This is work for a qualified electrician, and the device datasheet has the final word. Our breaker choices are on the Our picks page.
